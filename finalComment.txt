I think the experiment succeeded—but with a more nuanced conclusion than “GPU SIMD is faster.”

  The strongest result is that the article’s execution model genuinely applies to vector rendering. Analytic coverage scanning maps naturally onto GPU subgroups, and the real shuffleUp
  prefix scan worked correctly on both 32-lane Intel and 64-lane Radeon hardware. This was not merely GPU-shaped scalar code; it used actual cross-lane subgroup operations.

  What we learned:

  - CPU SIMD and GPU subgroup SIMD can express the same logical lane algorithm, but they are not the same execution machinery.
  - The subgroup scan was materially better than shared memory on Intel, but mostly tied on Radeon. Subgroup primitives are useful, not universally optimal.
  - Scan-only work is often too small to justify GPU overhead. The Intel CPU won at 512² and 1024² and crossed over only around 2048².
  - Painting changes the result because it adds parallel pixel work while coverage stays resident. Both GPUs then won across the measured 512²–2048² range.
  - Sparse tiles mattered more than another small scan optimization. Avoiding dense materialization and a 16 MiB upload produced a larger practical benefit than shaving a few
    instructions from the prefix scan.

  - Batching several draws mainly reduced submission and synchronization overhead. It did not make the subgroup arithmetic itself faster.
  - The final renderer result survived construction, upload, composition, and final readback: 3.16× on the Intel UHD 620 system and 2.66× on the Radeon 8060S system versus persistent
    all-core AVX2.

  So, is running SIMD on the GPU worthwhile? Yes, when:

  - there are enough pixels or draws;
  - lane communication is regular, such as scans and reductions;
  - input is compact;
  - intermediate coverage and pixels stay GPU-resident;
  - synchronization occurs per batch or frame, not per path.

  It is less attractive for tiny shapes, scan-only microkernels, frequent CPU readback, or workloads with substantial CPU preprocessing and little pixel work.

  The experiment also exposed an important portability lesson: reported subgroup width was not enough. Intel initially created two smaller execution subgroups inside the workgroup.
  Correctness required subgroup-size control and explicitly requiring a full subgroup. Any system built around “GPU SIMD” must treat lane width and execution guarantees as part of the
  contract.

  The main limitation is that this still does not prove superiority over production Blend2D. Our CPU analytic renderer is good enough as a controlled AVX2 reference, but Blend2D has
  years of optimization: persistent worker pools, clipping, command batching, sophisticated edge storage, compositing specializations, and cache-aware pipelines. The experiment
  supports building a Blend2D-inspired GPU backend; it does not demonstrate that it would beat Blend2D for every real scene.

  My overall judgment: the article’s core abstraction holds. The performance claim only holds after changing the question from “is one GPU SIMD scan faster?” to “can a sparse analytic
  rendering pipeline keep enough useful work resident on the GPU?” For that second, more realistic question, our answer is clearly yes.

  External review (After reading README, PLAN, RESULTS, the scan shader, and the CPU lane program)

  Overall: as an engineering artifact and a measurement study this is strong and
  unusually honest. As a test of the VectorWare article it lands as "the
  abstraction is real; the performance case for it is narrow, and the money is in
  the layer the abstraction doesn't touch."

  What's strong:

  - The methodology has better hygiene than most published GPU work. The three
    questions are properly separated, and the CPU baseline is all-core AVX2
    (MIMD x SIMD), not single-thread, which is the honest control most "GPU wins"
    quietly avoid. Device timestamp vs submit+fence vs readback are distinguished
    and readback cost is not hidden. Medians and p90, software-renderer
    rejection, CSV schema versioning, byte-exact reference checks with invariant
    tests.
  - The subgroup-size-control bug is the crown jewel. Intel reporting a nominal
    subgroup of 32 while splitting a 32-thread workgroup into two execution
    subgroups (seams at x=32) until VK_EXT_subgroup_size_control + require-full-
    subgroups is exactly the "lane width and execution guarantees are part of the
    contract" lesson. It was hit in practice, not read from a spec.
  - The Blend2D grounding is real: the combined cover/area cell identity,
    even-odd/non-zero folds, 8-bit subpixel quantization, sparse 64x16 tiles,
    and direct compact-tile scan carrying signed carry through inactive tiles.
    The ScalarOps/Avx2Ops trait over one coverageProgram<Ops> template
    structurally guarantees the scalar and AVX2 controls run identical logic.

  The sharp part: the data quietly undercuts the article's framing. The article's
  distinctive claim is the cross-lane one (a warp is an addressable vector; write
  SIMD, get GPU speed). Here the cross-lane shuffle scan was the least decisive
  ingredient: modest and architecture-dependent (beats shared memory on Intel,
  ties or loses on Radeon at 2048). The actual wins came from things the article
  does not emphasize: eliminating dense materialization, shrinking upload
  (3.7-14x), keeping coverage resident, and massively parallel paint. Those are
  generic GPU-programming concerns, not SIMD-lane portability. The honest framing
  is a conditional rebuttal dressed as a conditional yes: the programming model
  maps cleanly, but the performance story is "GPU + sparse data + residency,"
  which ordinary SIMT already delivers.

  One methodological soft spot worth stating out loud: the scan is framed as a
  full-width parallel prefix scan, the one shape CPUs are worst at and GPUs are
  best at. Production Blend2D resolves cells with a sparse-sequential sweep over
  active cells, which is nearly free because most cells are zero. So "GPU
  subgroup scan beats our AVX2 dense scan" is partly a consequence of the
  framing, not just Blend2D's years of tuning. Relatedly, subgroupScan() is
  parallel within a subgroup but serial across chunks via blockCarry (at width
  2048 / 64 lanes, 32 serialized carry steps per row), which is almost certainly
  why shared memory catches up at 2048 on Radeon.

  Suggested next steps: (1) add a sparse-sequential CPU cell scan as the real
  Blend2D-style control -- if the GPU still wins against that, the result gets
  much stronger; (2) lead the RESULTS conclusion with the residency finding,
  since the data supports it cleanly and it is the more valuable insight;
  (3) a discrete GPU with dedicated VRAM would sharpen the upload/residency
  story, which both shared-memory integrated GPUs currently soften.

  Two clarifications that matter most for reading the results table correctly,
  and that the summary above glosses over:

  1. The brute-force "distributed GPU" path is NOT a test of the article's
     distinctive claim. One pixel per invocation is ordinary SIMT with zero
     cross-lane operations; it validates only that the lane program runs on the
     GPU, which is trivially true. The single place the article's actual claim
     (a warp as an addressable vector, using shuffle/ballot) is exercised is the
     coverage/analytic scan milestone. A reader skimming the final matrix could
     easily credit the article for the brute-force point-in-path speedups, which
     have nothing to do with cross-lane execution. Keeping that distinction sharp
     is what makes the study honest: only the shuffle scan is on trial, and it
     came back "useful, architecture-dependent," not "faster CPU SIMD."

  2. The through-line to the article critique is that lane-per-thread SIMD is
     nearly orthogonal to where the GPU wins actually come from. The article
     pitches "write portable SIMD, the warp is your vector unit." But every
     decisive win here -- sparse tiles, smaller upload, resident coverage,
     parallel paint, batched submission -- lives in the tiling / data-movement /
     residency layer that a Simd<T,N> abstraction does not express at all. The
     cross-lane primitive it does express turned out to be the marginal factor.
     So the practical lesson is the inverse of the article's emphasis: the SIMD
     mapping is the easy, portable, and least important part; the GPU-specific
     memory and scheduling layer is where the engineering and the speed live.
     That is not a refutation of the mapping -- it maps cleanly and correctly --
     but it relocates the value from "SIMD portability" to "GPU-resident sparse
     pipelines," which is a different and more demanding discipline.
